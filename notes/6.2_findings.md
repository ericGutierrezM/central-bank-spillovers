# Section 6.2 Findings

## Purpose

This note tracks what `notebooks/6.2_predict_llm_disagreement.ipynb` currently establishes, what exact data and methods each claim comes from, and how the section should fit conceptually into the paper.

The goal of Section 6.2 is **not** to replace the earlier result that different LLMs can generate different causal estimates. Instead, it helps explain **when and why** LLM choice is most likely to matter.

---

## Conceptual Role In The Paper

### What the paper already shows before 6.2

- Different LLMs classify the same communication differently.
- These differences carry through the orthogonalization step into different residual communication shocks.
- In some cases, those differences propagate into different spillover coefficients and even different significance conclusions.

### What 6.2 adds

- LLM disagreement is **systematic and text-linked**, not random noise.
- The settings that generate disagreement are interpretable.
- In particular, disagreement concentrates in passages that seem to stress the **neutral-versus-stanced boundary**, especially when the text mixes hawkish and dovish signals.

### Best current framing

- The earlier results establish that **LLM choice can matter downstream**.
- Section 6.2 helps explain **where that sensitivity comes from**.
- The right conceptual claim is:

> LLM disagreement is an indicator of communications in which the neutral-versus-stanced boundary is genuinely contested, and therefore where model choice is more likely to become consequential.

Important caution:
- We should **not** claim that 6.2 proves a fully non-circular causal chain from text disagreement to shock disagreement.
- The residual shocks are built from LLM-specific stance labels, so some variation in shocks is expected by construction.
- The value of 6.2 is therefore to explain the **textual conditions** under which this upstream sensitivity arises.

## Definitions Used

### Main disagreement target

`split`

Definition:
- a turn is `split = 1` if at least one LLM is `neutral`
- and at least one other LLM is directional (`dovish`, `mostly dovish`, `mostly hawkish`, `hawkish`)

Interpretation:
- this is the cleanest operationalization of the main paper result that disagreement lives mostly at the **neutral-versus-stanced boundary**

### Secondary disagreement target

`high_std3`

Definition:
- compute the cross-LLM standard deviation after mapping labels to `{-1, 0, +1}`
- classify a turn as `high_std3 = 1` if its value is in the top quartile

Interpretation:
- unusually high cross-model instability on a common three-point stance scale

### Mechanism features

The notebook also constructs simple text-side indicators:

- `mixed_signal`
  - the turn contains both hawkish and dovish lexicon matches
- `inflation_present`
  - the turn contains inflation/prices language
- `hedge_rate`
  - normalized frequency of hedge/uncertainty language
- `fg_rate`
  - normalized frequency of forward-guidance style phrases
- `word_count_z`
  - standardized turn length

## Main Predictive Findings

### Claim

Disagreement is meaningfully predictable from the text itself, and flexible text features outperform a fixed policy dictionary.

### Method

- 5-fold meeting-grouped cross-validation
- binary classification evaluated with ROC-AUC
- two text representations:
  - **TF-IDF**
  - **Gorodnichenko-style dictionary** from `literature/Gorodnichenko.md`

### Exact results

Source:
- `output/stance/disagreement_simple_summary.csv`

For `split`:
- TF-IDF pooled ROC-AUC = **0.755**
- Gorodnichenko pooled ROC-AUC = **0.654**

For `high_std3`:
- TF-IDF pooled ROC-AUC = **0.744**
- Gorodnichenko pooled ROC-AUC = **0.647**

### Interpretation

- disagreement is not random
- text alone contains useful signal about when LLMs will diverge
- a simple hawk/dove dictionary captures some of that signal, but not enough
- broader textual composition matters more than isolated policy words

## Main Mechanism Findings

### Claim

Disagreement is especially concentrated in passages with mixed policy signals, which is consistent with the idea that models diverge primarily over whether a passage is directional enough to move off neutral.

### Method

Descriptive exposure table:
- compare disagreement rates across simple binary text conditions

Statistical test:
- two-proportion test for the `split` rate
- report percentage-point difference, 95% CI, and p-value

Sources:
- `output/stance/disagreement_simple_mechanism_exposures.csv`
- `output/stance/disagreement_simple_mechanism_tests.csv`

### Exact results: mixed hawk+dove language

Exposure:
- `No mixed signal`: `23.7%` split
- `Mixed hawk+dove`: `47.6%` split

Test:
- difference = **+23.9 percentage points**
- 95% CI = **[20.7, 27.0]** percentage points
- `p < 0.001`

### Exact results: inflation/prices language

Exposure:
- `No inflation/prices`: `21.6%` split
- `Inflation/prices present`: `45.7%` split

Test:
- difference = **+24.1 percentage points**
- 95% CI = **[21.1, 27.0]** percentage points
- `p < 0.001`

### Length pattern

The notebook also shows that disagreement rises with turn length:
- shortest decile: split rate about `3.8%`
- longest decile: split rate about `41.9%`

Interpretation:
- longer turns give more room for disagreement
- but length alone does not seem to be the whole story
- mixed signals and inflation language appear to capture a more substantive mechanism

## How These Findings Fit The Existing Neutral Result

### Potential confusion

At first glance, the mixed-signal result can sound like it conflicts with the earlier paper claim that LLMs do **not** mostly disagree on hawkish versus dovish direction.

### Resolution

The correct reading is:
- mixed hawk+dove language does **not** mainly create direct hawk-versus-dove reversals
- instead, it creates ambiguity about whether the passage is directional **at all**

In other words:
- one model may net the passage to neutral
- another may judge that one side dominates enough to call it hawkish or dovish

So the mixed-signal result is actually consistent with the core finding:

> disagreement is concentrated at the neutral-versus-stanced boundary

Best short line:

> Mixed-signal passages do not primarily create directional reversals; they primarily stress the neutral threshold.

## Exact Paper-Safe Claims Supported Right Now

### Strong claims

- Different LLMs disagree systematically rather than randomly.
- This disagreement is text-predictable.
- TF-IDF substantially outperforms a fixed Gorodnichenko-style dictionary in predicting disagreement.
- Passages that mix hawkish and dovish signals are much more disagreement-prone.
- Inflation/prices passages are also much more disagreement-prone.
- These patterns are statistically strong in simple percentage comparisons.

### Claims to phrase carefully

- We should not say that 6.2 proves that disagreement causes downstream causal instability by itself.
- The orthogonalized shocks are partially downstream of the LLM labels by construction.
- So 6.2 is best used as a **mechanism/explanatory section**, not as the sole bridge to the causal results.
